You type a website address, press enter, and in an instant the page appears. It looks simple, yet there is a long journey of data behind it. To understand it, let's start from the very basics: how two computers can talk to each other.

## Two computers, one path

A website is stored on another computer called a **server**. Your computer (or phone) requests data, and the server sends back the answer. Both communicate over a network, which can use cables or wireless connections like Wi-Fi or cellular signals.

Think of it like a highway: roads connect your house to your destination. Without roads, you can't go anywhere; without a network, data can't move.

## What if there are many computers?

Connecting two computers is easy: just one line. But if you want to connect to many computers, you would have to run separate lines to each one. The more computers there are, the more tangled and expensive it gets. Just like if every house in a city had to have its own private road leading to every other house.

The solution is a **network switch**, a device that acts like a central terminal. Every computer simply connects to the switch, and the switch forwards data to the target computer. Switches can also connect to other switches, allowing a network to keep growing—for example, across an entire school building or office.

## From small networks to the internet

Even a building-sized network isn't enough to reach the whole world. That's why these smaller networks are connected to one another to form a giant network. Its full name is *interconnected network*, and you already know its abbreviation: the **internet**.

There are two important things that connect your home network to the outside world:

- **Modem**: a device that translates your home network's signal so it can travel over your service provider's lines, whether telephone cables, fiber optics, or cellular networks.
- **ISP (Internet Service Provider)**: the company that provides internet service. ISPs operate **routers**, devices that determine which direction data should be forwarded, and those routers are interconnected with those of other ISPs. To cross continents, most data travels through undersea fiber-optic cables.

## The journey of data, in brief

Your device, then your home router or Wi-Fi, then the modem, then your ISP, then another ISP's network, until it finally reaches the destination server. The reply from the server returns along a similar path. All of this happens in a matter of seconds—often even less.
