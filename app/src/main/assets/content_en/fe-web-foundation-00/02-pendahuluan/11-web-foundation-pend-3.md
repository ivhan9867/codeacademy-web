Almost every day, you open websites. Yet the terms "website" and "web page" are often used interchangeably, even though they are different. Let's distinguish them.

## Web page

A **web page** is a single page displaying information: text, images, videos, or other media. That page can also be made interactive with JavaScript code, such as buttons that react when clicked or forms that validate input before submitting.

How can all those elements appear in a browser? There are specific building blocks behind them, and we will explore them in the Website Anatomy lesson.

## Website

A **website** is a collection of interconnected web pages. What connects them are **hyperlinks**, links that take you from one page to another, either within the same domain or to a different domain.

An analogy is a book: a web page is a single sheet of paper, while a website is the whole book, complete with a table of contents and cross-references between pages.

The contents of a website are usually managed by an individual, group, or organization, and all of its files are stored on a **server**. We will discuss servers in the next lesson.

## The web is not the internet

These two terms often get mixed up. The **internet** is the network infrastructure, like a highway. The **web** is one of the services running on top of the internet, like a vehicle driving on that road. Email apps or online games also use the internet without necessarily going through the web.

## A brief history

In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working at CERN, proposed the idea of the **World Wide Web (WWW)**. His original goal was simple: to make it easier for scientists at various universities and research institutes to share information. The web was then opened to the public in the early 1990s.

To preserve that first website, CERN launched a restoration project in 2013. You can still explore how it looked at `info.cern.ch`, and you will see how simple it was: just text and links.

## The web today

Today, the web goes far beyond sharing news. Through a browser alone, you can chat in real time, play music, edit documents collaboratively, and even edit photos and videos—for example, with online photo editors like Pixlr. Many apps that previously had to be installed can now simply be opened in a browser. That is one reason the skill of building for the web is increasingly in demand.
